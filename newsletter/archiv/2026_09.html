Welcome to the fourth issue of the MERRI Community Newsletter

From the editors

Welcome back to the MERRI community after the summer break!

Inspired by first-hand experiences and discussions during and following this summer’s iEMSs (International Environmental Modelling & Software society) conference in Dublin, we would like to open this issue by reflecting on a development that is gaining momentum across research fields: the move towards shared standards for transparent, reproducible and reusable modelling.

Discussions took place beyond the conference sessions themselves, for example during the 2026 Open Modeling Foundation (OMF) Members’ Council Meeting. A unanimous stance among attendees was the growing recognition of the need for shared standards and best practices. The OMF’s work aims to make computational models more accessible, understandable, reusable and interoperable across disciplines and modelling traditions.

For those of us working in environmental modelling, this direction feels both familiar and significant. The ODD (Overview, Design Concepts, Details) protocol was originally developed to document agent-based ecological models, but the method has since been adopted and extended across disciplines and model types. Its evolution demonstrates how an approach originating in a single, specific research community can grow and reach other disciplines, gradually creating a broader shared language for describing, assessing and reusing diverse types of models.

This work signals a broader movement. Communities that have often developed their own conventions independently are increasingly turning towards shared descriptions and interoperable resources, creating resources useful for different applications or even disciplines. Environmental modelling clearly values and contributes to this wider movement, but can this ethos be adopted by other disciplines?

This also raises a question for MERRI colleagues: what information about our work - and in how much detail - must we document and share for others to genuinely understand, evaluate and reuse it? Does this differ for sharing within our discipline or sharing more broadly? We would be very interested to hear how these challenges appear in your own field.

So, if you have further comments and thoughts about this topic, we encourage you to respond to this email.

Also, other ideas or suggestions regarding forthcoming newsletter issues are welcome, feel free to submit content to cover in our next edition!

Recent highlights from the field

Changes to OSF – The Center for Open Science (COS)

COS has announced a major change for The Open Science Framework (OSF), a popular open platform and repository for documenting, managing, and sharing research data. The move will remove several main functionalities (such as OSF Projects, data and code sharing, and private storage), to reduce and streamline its model and focus on integration with other platforms like Zenodo. This move can be explained in part by funding difficulties threatening platform sustainability. Existing projects will not be affected, but users will be unable to create or make substantial changes to projects from November 16th, 2026 and projects will become read-only after February 19th, 2027. This will likely greatly impact a lot of researchers’ workflows, and users are being encouraged to explore alternative platforms like Zenodo, Dryad, Dataverse, and Figshare, depending on required functionalities.

More information here:

Community submissions

A new journal for metascience: Journal of Research on Research

Colleagues from the MERRI community have helped launch the Journal of Research on Research (J·ROR), a new international, peer-reviewed, open-access journal dedicated to understanding and improving how research is funded, organised, conducted, communicated and evaluated. Its broad scope includes topics such as metascience, Open Science, research integrity, peer review, research assessment and management, scientometrics, research culture, among other related fields. The journal welcomes empirical and conceptual contributions using qualitative, quantitative, mixed-methods, or simulation-based approaches. The editorial team particularly encourages members of the MERRI community to consider J·ROR for their future submissions.

More information and submissions: https://www.tandfonline.com/journals/rror20/about-this-journal

MERRI-ly Scientific

505 ways to say “almost significant”

How creatively can researchers describe a result that does not cross the conventional p < 0.05 threshold? A meta-research study screened 567,758 randomised controlled trials published over 30 years for 505 such expressions. At least one was found in 49,134 articles (8.65%). The most popular examples included “marginally significant,” “all but significant,” and “a nonsignificant trend.” Among the p-values examined manually, the median was (perhaps unsurprisingly) 0.06.

A gentle reminder that exact p-values, effect sizes and confidence intervals are usually more informative than creative ways of approaching significance.

Source: Otte, W. M., Vinkers, C. H., Habets, P. C., van IJzendoorn, D. G. P., & Tijdink, J. K. (2022). Analysis of 567,758 randomized controlled trials published over 30 years reveals trends in phrases used to discuss results that do not reach statistical significance. PLOS Biology, 20(2), e3001562, https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.3001562
